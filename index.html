Who can afford Portland?

Nobody knows exactly what rents will be in 20 years. So instead of making one forecast, this model plays out the future hundreds of times, each with different random luck: recessions, demand swings, wage shocks, and uncertainty about how the housing market itself behaves. The spread of outcomes shows what's likely, what's possible, and which policies change the odds.

How it works

What is a Monte Carlo simulation?

A Monte Carlo simulation answers "what will happen?" when the answer depends on things that are random or unknown. Instead of guessing one value for each unknown, you draw random values, run the model, record the result, and repeat many times. The collection of results is an estimate of the full probability distribution of outcomes. It's named after the Monte Carlo casino because it works like rolling dice over and over.

1Draw the unknowns

Each run picks its own values for 7 uncertain market parameters.

2Simulate year by year

Each year, random shocks hit demand, wages, rents, and the economy. Households are displaced or move in.

3Repeat

500 runs per policy, so 2,500 simulated futures in total.

4Summarize

Medians, percentile bands, and probabilities are computed from the pile of results.

Fair comparisons. Run #k uses exactly the same random numbers under every policy (a technique called common random numbers). If run #37 has a recession in year 6 under Status Quo, it has the same recession under Zoning Reform. So differences between policies come from the policy, not from luck. Every result is reproducible: the same seed always gives the same numbers.

Two kinds of uncertainty

1. Random events (aleatory uncertainty) are things that are random by nature: whether a recession hits, how wages move, how popular a neighborhood gets. These are drawn fresh every year.

2. Unknown parameters (epistemic uncertainty) are fixed facts about how the market works that we just don't know precisely, like how strongly rents respond to a shortage. Each run draws one value for each from a triangular distribution (low, most likely, high) and keeps it for all years. Including both makes the uncertainty bands honest.

The small histograms show the actual values drawn across all runs in the last simulation.

The model, one year at a time

The city is split into 5 neighborhoods (n) and 4 income groups (g). Each year t, the model runs steps A–H. Variables with an orange highlight are random draws.

A. Is the economy in a recession?

The economy is a two-state Markov chain. A recession starts with probability $\lambda$ (an uncertain parameter) and continues with probability 0.35.

$$P(R_t = 1 \mid R_{t-1}=0) = \lambda, \qquad P(R_t = 1 \mid R_{t-1}=1) = 0.35$$

Recessions cut demand growth by 1 point, income growth by 3 points, and construction starts by 40%.

B. Housing demand

$D_{g,n,t}$ is the number of group-$g$ households that want to live in neighborhood $n$. It grows with the neighborhood's popularity, plus random citywide and local shocks, and shrinks when rents outpace inflation:

$$D_{g,n,t} = D_{g,n,t-1}\Big(1 + \mu_n + a_g + \color{#f0883e}{\varepsilon^{\text{city}}_t} + \color{#f0883e}{\varepsilon^{\text{local}}_{n,t}} - \eta\, r_{n,t-1} - 0.01R_t\Big)$$

$\mu_n$ = baseline demand growth, $a_g$ = group adjustment (higher earners growing faster), $r_{n,t-1}$ = last year's real rent growth, $\eta$ = demand elasticity, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 0.005^2)$.

C. Incomes

$$Y_{g,n,t} = Y_{g,n,t-1}\Big(1 + \gamma_g + \color{#f0883e}{\omega_t} + \color{#f0883e}{\omega_{g,n,t}} - 0.03R_t\Big), \qquad \omega \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 0.012^2)$$

$Y_{g,n,t}$ is the group's median income. Incomes vary within each group too (lognormal with spread $\sigma$). The model represents that spread with $K=25$ evenly spaced income levels:

$$y_{k} = Y_{g,n,t}\, e^{\sigma z_k}, \qquad z_k = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{k+0.5}{K}\right), \quad k = 0,\dots,24$$

This means "share of households" can be computed directly: each income level stands for 1/25 = 4% of the group.

D. Construction (with a 2-year pipeline)

Builders start more projects when rents are rising, but zoning limits how much they can build. Projects started now are finished in 2 years:

$$\text{Starts}_{n,t} = U_{n,t}\cdot c_n \cdot \underbrace{\big(1 - 0.5\, Z_n (1-\rho_z)\big)}_{\text{zoning}} \cdot \max(0,\ 1 + \phi\, r_{n,t-1}) \cdot \kappa \cdot (1 - 0.4R_t)$$ $$U_{n,t} = U_{n,t-1} + \text{Starts}_{n,t-2}$$

$U$ = market units, $c_n$ = base construction rate, $Z_n \in [0,1]$ = zoning restrictiveness, $\rho_z$ = policy's zoning relief, $\phi$ = supply response, $\kappa$ = policy construction multiplier. Public units are built on the same 2-year lag and filled by low-income households who pay 30% of income.

E. Displacement

Rent burden for each income level is $b_k = 12P_{n,t}/y_k$. The yearly chance of being forced out rises with burden above 30%:

$$p_{g,n,t} = s_g\cdot\tau\cdot\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\min\!\Big(0.002 + \beta\max(b_k - 0.30,\ 0),\ 0.15\Big)$$ $$\color{#f0883e}{\text{Displaced}_{g,n,t} \sim \text{Binomial}\big(H_{g,n,t},\ p_{g,n,t}\big)}$$

$s_g$ = how sensitive the group is to cost pressure (1.0 for low income, 0.1 for high), $\tau$ = the policy's displacement multiplier, scaled by how well protections work: $\tau = 1-(1-\tau_{\text{policy}})\,e$. Each household is effectively a coin flip with probability $p$; the binomial distribution counts how many "lose".

F. Who moves in

Landlords require income of at least 3× the rent. The share of each group that qualifies is:

$$q_{g,n,t} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k \mathbf{1}\big[y_k \ge 3\cdot 12P_{n,t}\big]$$

Empty units (keeping 3% for normal turnover) go to waiting households in proportion to how many qualify, $W_{g,n,t}\,q_{g,n,t}$, where $W = \max(D - H, 0)$ is unmet demand. This is where gentrification happens: displaced low-income households are replaced mostly by higher earners.

G. Rents

Rents rise with effective demand (people who live there or could qualify to) relative to supply, measured against a healthy 5% vacancy:

$$\text{Tightness}_{n,t} = \frac{H^{\text{mkt}}_{n,t} + \sum_g W_{g,n,t}\,q_{g,n,t}}{U_{n,t}} - 0.95$$ $$P_{n,t} = P_{n,t-1}\Big(1 + \pi + \theta\cdot\text{Tightness}_{n,t} + \color{#f0883e}{\varepsilon^{\text{rent}}_{n,t}}\Big), \qquad \pi = 2.5\%,\ \ \varepsilon^{\text{rent}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 0.015^2)$$

Yearly rent growth is capped between −10% and +25%.

H. Record

The model records city rent (weighted by households), the share paying more than 30% of income, cumulative displacement, the low-income share, vacancy, and housing units. Public-housing tenants pay exactly 30%, so they don't count as burdened.

Turning thousands of runs into answers

With $S$ runs producing outcomes $x_1, \dots, x_S$:

Median and percentiles$$\hat{q}_{p} = \text{the value below which } p\% \text{ of runs fall}$$
Probability of an event$$\hat{P}(X > c) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbf{1}[x_s > c]$$
Precision of the estimate$$\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{s_x}{\sqrt{S}}, \quad \text{95\% CI} = \bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$$

The standard error shrinks with $\sqrt{S}$: 4× more runs makes the estimate 2× more precise. The Convergence section shows this happening.

Limitations

  • Illustrative inputs. Starting rents, households, units, incomes, and growth rates are reasonable-looking assumptions, not measured Portland data. Treat the dollar amounts as a demonstration; the ranking of policies and the shape of the trade-offs are the more meaningful output.
  • Policy effects are assumptions. The 55% displacement reduction from tenant protections and the 10% construction penalty are modeling choices. Research on rent control (e.g. Diamond, McQuade & Qian, 2019, on San Francisco) finds it protects existing tenants but shrinks rental supply; the exact sizes here are not estimated from Portland.
  • Neighborhoods don't interact. Displaced households leave the model instead of moving to another neighborhood or nearby town.
  • One rent per neighborhood. Real neighborhoods have cheap and expensive units side by side.
  • Parameter ranges are judgment calls. The triangular distributions express plausible ranges, not estimates fitted to data. The sensitivity section shows which of these matter most, which is where better data would help.
  • No funding constraints. Public housing and protections are assumed to be paid for; their cost and how it's raised aren't modeled.
